About

An age-old moral fable places Hercules at the challenging crossroads of choices: the easy life of pleasure and the hard life of virtue. Aristotle and Kant, two distinguished philosophers from distinct time-periods, unanimously claim that the hard life of virtue alone is a worthy life. Both justify virtuous living by tracing its necessary roots back to the central core of human nature – our rationality. Yet, as has been long supposed, the disagreements between their ethical positions are conspicuously loud: Aristotle favors happiness as the highest end of virtue, while Kant considers virtue to involve duty unadulterated by happiness; Kant favors a life of action towards a human world under moral laws, while Aristotle glorifies the life of contemplation as an ideal life of an individual. Whose position is valid and tenable? How do we account for the differences in their rationalist accounts of goodness? This course aims at a critical and comparative exploration of the moral theories proposed by Aristotle and Kant. It will begin with an introduction to the broader themes and trends in the history of moral philosophy to locate Aristotle and Kant in their respective contexts. It will discuss the metaethical underpinnings of their respective moral theories: Aristotle founds his eudaimonian virtue ethics on his commitments to a purpose-driven natural world (in Physics) and to the psychology of human soul (in De Anima). Kant on the other hand derives his deontological ethics from a critique of the faculty of reason, and by grounding our morality in the idea of freedom (in the first Critique and Groundwork III). The central focus of the course will be to address the dialectic of Aristotelian ‘happiness’ and Kantian ‘duty’ through an exploration of their respective ethical theories. Students will be introduced to the central themes of Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics and Eudemian Ethics including the notions of virtue (aretē), happiness (eudaimonia) and practical wisdom (phronēsis). They will also be introduced to Kant’s formulations of the moral law and the taxonomy of moral duties through selections from his Groundwork and Metaphysics of Morals. The course will also highlight the significance of Aristotle’s and Kant’s moral theories to enable students to reflect on the relevance of their ideas in today’s world.
