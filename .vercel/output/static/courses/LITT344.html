About

This course looks at the critical moments of encounter between two defining intimations of public space — cinema and mass politics — without collapsing the autonomy of cinema as an art, and politics as an hermeneutic of collective self-determination. Cinema’s birth at the summit of industrial modernity is no news, and neither is its capacity for both mass enrichment and manipulation. But there is relentless and often renewed debate about ‘political cinema’ and its ‘uses’, which lead us to the very genesis of the idea of mass culture in the early years of modernity. It must be stated at the very onset that the course looks at ‘narrative cinema’ rather than on documentary, cinéma vérité, or at the visual cultures of Lettrists, Situationists, avant-gardists, surrealists etc., all of whom have done pioneering work in the domain. How did cinema capture the political zeitgeist of the 1920s, 1940s and 1960s? Or even after? On the other hand, how does politics - as a thought and practice - allow for cinematic/spectacular enunciation? Habermas scholar Robert Porter pointedly asks, “Does cinema express or engender political thought? Can we think of cinema, or certain specific cinematic texts, as bodies of political theory?” His is an assertive nod. As an undergraduate course, this one would not set the high ambitions of teaching political theory through cinema. Therefore, the course does not name itself ‘political cinema’. There are, if one could say so, as many political films as there are kinds of politics, but more importantly, unlike other ‘studio-defined’ and ‘market-refined’ genres like ‘horror’, ‘crime’, ‘musical’, and ‘social’, political cinema has remained notoriously undefined (and undefinable). The way Soviet Cinema was ‘political’ is not the way James Bond Cold War spoofs were, nor were the French or Czech New Wave; or say, Satyajit Ray’s political fables made for children. But they are all political in the wide sense of the term. Hence, this course would not like to begin with an apriori idea of ‘political cinema’ but rather inquire if such a conceptual framework emerges through and with the course. To that end, the course would follow in the steps of Walter Benjamin, the Frankfurt School, Third Cinema and its legacies, and postcolonial uses of cinema. Claire Molloy and Yannis Tzioumakis, in The Routledge Companion To Cinema And Politics says that cinema “remains central to the formation and transformation of geopolitical discourses, imagining new geographies and connecting (or separating), in complex ways, the concepts of boundaries and territories with ideas about nation and identity.” At the same time, cinema has often found expressing working class and feminist solidarity as much as it has bored into the pervasive and pernicious nature of political memory, politics as stimulus, and the abiding lust for tyranny in political regimes. These will be some of the lines of argument/reflection in this course.
