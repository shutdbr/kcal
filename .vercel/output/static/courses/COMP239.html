About

How do computers &quot;see&quot; the world? The mathematical foundation of computer vision lies in understanding how shapes and images transform—and this course will teach you that language from the ground up. Starting with the familiar geometry of high school (Euclidean), we&#39;ll discover how different types of transformations create entirely new geometries. You&#39;ll learn how allowing shapes to be scaled gives us Similarity Geometry, how parallel lines behave in Affine Geometry, and ultimately how Projective Geometry explains why railroad tracks appear to meet at the horizon—the same mathematics that lets computers reconstruct 3D scenes from 2D photographs. This isn&#39;t your typical geometry course. Following Felix Klein&#39;s revolutionary approach, we&#39;ll see geometry not as a collection of theorems about triangles and circles, but as the study of what stays the same when shapes transform. This unified view connects ancient Greek mathematics to cutting-edge computer vision applications like facial recognition, autonomous vehicles, and augmented reality. No advanced mathematics required—just high school algebra, basic calculus, and curiosity about how mathematical ideas connect. Through concrete examples and visual reasoning, you&#39;ll build intuition for concepts that usually appear only in graduate courses. This course sets the foundation for Computer Vision II, where you&#39;ll apply these geometric tools to real-world vision problems.
