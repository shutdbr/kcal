About

Materials have shaped history and our everyday lives. The social, political, and economic structures determining access to materials create varying environmental outcomes. Environment outcomes in turn shape societies and its practices over time via the provisioning of materials. Our current thinking about sustainability, conservation, and environmental crises are all largely shaped by the ramifications of our material relationships. While philosophers have expounded on the dialectic tensions between the material and the ideal as an intrinsic feature of the human condition for a long time now, only relatively recently have the implications of this dialectic tension been explored by scholars of the environmental studies to better understand environmental and social concerns, both of the present-day (overproduction, consumer culture, social-ecological fallout), and leading into the future (New Materialism). This course is designed to be a whirlwind tour of the various ways to think about environmental and social contexts underpinning various kinds of materialisms. Past and present cautionary tales of humanity’s precarious relationship with the material world ⎯the bedrock upon which the dialectic of the material and the ideal hinges on⎯ have culminated in ecological collapse and social strife. However, political, historical, and economic contexts complicate these simplistic cautionary tales to a large extent with regards to causality. Lastly, materials are what nourishes our body and our mind, and brings joy to our lives; we delight in the textures, noises, colours, and tastes of things. Discussions on future materialisms could be much less likely about how we value material things too much, but rather about how we don’t value them enough (Thorpe 2014).
