About

The human “propensity to truck, barter, and exchange” (a la Adam Smith) lies at the heart of all modern economies. And yet, social scientists have adopted widely diverging theoretical frameworks to understand how market economies function and evolve. At one end, the dominant strand of economics today takes rational and instrumental action by individuals as its starting point when modelling market exchange. By contrast, an equally influential stream of social thought assumes that economic behaviour is conditioned by the same set of social and political institutions that also govern other types of non-economic behaviour. In this course, students will be introduced to the latter set of intellectual traditions in political economy and economic sociology, wherein “institutions” are treated not merely as constraints upon economic behaviour but also as constitutive of both the preferences and norms that inform such behaviour. The course is divided into two modules. The first module (four weeks) revisits the work of some original thinkers to locate the intellectual origins of this field of study. The second module poses a series of comparative and historical questions that help illustrate the evolution of the capitalist world economy from the 18th to the 21st century. Some of the possible themes to be covered in the second module will include: the great divergence, industrialization and financialization, inequality and social im/mobility, mass education and the establishment of professions, tax reforms and welfare states, and new social movements.
