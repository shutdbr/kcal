About

The growth versus environment debate has been one of India’s nay the global community’s major policy dilemmas for over a century now, exacerbated in the era of global warming and climate change. Further deepening the dilemma are the processes set off by globalization, privatization and liberalization combined with the role of technology in people’s lives. Yet, this debate has also spurred a new line of thinking and public policies in recent times. Growth is an imperative for material needs or poverty alleviation. But ecology, too, needs to be healthy, in the absence of which there’s a heavy price to be paid by the humanity, as witnessed in the unprecedented disasters: landslides, recurring floods and droughts, water scarcity or the devastation caused by extreme weather events and the society’s unpreparedness in such cases. It’s a quintessential “wicked problem” – to borrow from the term coined first in a 1973 paper by Horst W J Rittel and Melvin M Webber, professors of design and urban planning at the University of California at Berkeley (Dilemmas in a general theory of planning) – that does not allow for the best solution but rather only the acceptable compromises. Questions abound: Is there such thing called sustainable economic growth, as advocated by the different stakeholders? What’s the history and philosophy of environmentalism and the many different tensions centred around these two ideas? How does public policy factor in both? This course, drawing from field work and theoretical foundations, would offer students of social and environmental sciences with a keen interest in public policy a thorough review of this classical dilemma and the choices a society has and makes. From the earliest debates over the construction of large dams, to industrial development, to the ideas of green economy or degrowth, and the history of India’s environmental legislation, the week-by-week sessions would build on the different thematic aspects of this debate and pose before students the confounding questions integral to understanding a public policy dilemma. For instance, the social and economic fallout of the land acquisition for development projects by the State using the whip of Eminent Domain: What happens to the people who are displaced by development projects? This itself posits several questions for consideration: Is development possible without any environmental, ecological or social harm? Or is there a need for a paradigm shift to strike a balance between the two processes, as the world runs out of time to deal with and eventually fix the global poly-crisis, a term coined by historian Adam Toonze. With class-rooms discussions intermittent with lectures, presentations and anecdotal stories from the field, this course will dwell upon the theoretical foundations as well as the real-life situations to explore tensions between the cause of environmental conservation and preservation and an inevitability of development for economic growth.
