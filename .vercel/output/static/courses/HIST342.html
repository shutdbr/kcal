About

Modernism: The Art and Architecture of Anxiety, begins by exploring the pivotal artistic and architectural movements of the early 20th century, as attempts to find order and control in an increasingly chaotic world. A growing feeling of alienation and uncertainty about the future owing to the social upheaval of industrialization and the trauma of World War I prompted a widespread rejection of traditional artistic, social, and political norms in Euro- America. Modernism, in this “western” sense, was principally an exploration of new forms and techniques that shattered existing aesthetic modes, and established a new kind of truth or order that was centered around fragmentation. In the initial leg of the course students will therefore explore how the crisis of certainty in turn of twentieth century Europe, eventually led to an artistic embrace of abstraction. A sense of an intellectual and existential vacuum prompted artists to abandon representational art, as they felt that objective reality could no longer be accurately depicted. Instead, they embraced non-figurative art to express inner, subjective “truths” and new, non-Euclidean approaches towards comprehending space and time. In the second portion of this course we will examine how the anxiety undergirding the advent of Indian modernism was distinct from its Western counterpart. While European modernists grappled with the alienation of the industrial age and the fragmentation of reality, Indian artists faced a different, more complex set of concerns: the trauma of colonialism, the struggle for national identity, and the challenge of synthesizing tradition with modernity. In her influential book, &quot;When Was Modernism,&quot; the art critic Geeta Kapur, for instance, highlights how Indian modernists were not simply rebelling against a bourgeois establishment, but were instead engaged in a &quot;double discourse.&quot; The anxiety in this context was not about societal fragmentation but rather the existential struggle to forge a new national identity amidst the pressures of colonial rule. Indian modernists grappled with the tension between preserving cultural heritage and embracing the future, a creative anxiety that defined their artistic and architectural expressions. Drawing from Kapur’s writing, and from the art of Nasreen Mohamedi, and the architecture of Charles Correa and BV Doshi, the final leg of the course will therefore examine the extent to which abstraction in the context of Indian Modernism may be viewed as a profound effort to impose order on a world that appeared increasingly divided up between the national and the global, between the inner and the outer, and between the mind and the body.
