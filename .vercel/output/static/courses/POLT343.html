About

Faith, Freedom, and the Constitution: Religion and Law examines the relationship between religion and law in contemporary India through the constitutional governance of religious diversity. It explores how courts, legislatures, and administrative institutions define religion, protect religious freedom, regulate religious practices, and adjudicate conflicts involving equality, citizenship, caste, and religious identity. While centred on India, the course adopts a comparative perspective by engaging debates from the United States and Europe concerning secularism, religious liberty, legal pluralism, and minority rights. Organised around four foundational questions that have shaped some of the most significant constitutional controversies in modern India, the course first asks, under the theme of secularism: How should a constitutional state govern religion? It then examines, through the lens of religious freedom: What protections should individuals and communities enjoy? Turning to personal law and legal pluralism, it considers: Can religious diversity coexist with equal citizenship? Finally, through the study of caste and conversion, it investigates: How does law regulate religious identity and social hierarchy? Drawing on constitutional texts, judicial decisions, and interdisciplinary scholarship from law, political theory, history, sociology, and religious studies, the course encourages critical reflection on the challenges of governing religious diversity in democratic societies. Particular attention is paid to the ways in which constitutional commitments to liberty, equality, secularism, and minority protection intersect—and at times come into tension—in contemporary legal and political debates.
