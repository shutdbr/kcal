About

This interdisciplinary course prepares prospective academics, activists, ethicists, leaders, researchers and policy-makers for environmental justice challenges and opportunities in India and across the world. The initial course module focuses on the theoretical foundations and conceptual contexts of environmental justice. Next, an experiential education module guides learners in developing familiarity with the practice of environmental justice. The concluding module on institutional and societal changes for environmental justice further develops and synthesises the theoretical and practical insights generated earlier in the course. Environmental justice - a discourse that straddles research, policy, and activism – focuses on the dynamics of justice/injustice in environment-related thinking and action. Concepts closely linked to justice, for example - equity, equality, fairness, responsibility, difference, recognition, liberty, dignity, violence, goodness, rightness, etc. - necessarily get included in any analysis of environmental justice. A crude definitional attempt would be to say that environmental justice is concerned with who wins and who loses in any given environmental context, why and how this happens, why this matters to those involved and to all of us, and what can be done to change things around. Further, good environmental justice theory is attentive to questions of difference and colonialism and the multiple crises of contemporary global capitalism; some political thinkers, for example, explicitly identify the goals of green politics as striking at the very heart of the polluted, materialist, consumerist, unequal, and industrial capitalist society. Mainstream discourses of climate change, sustainability and sustainable development often obscure the injustice and inequity of specific kinds of human activity relating to the environment. Framing issues in the language of environmental justice therefore allows us to excavate these silences in the mainstream discourses of climate action and sustainability, which in turn makes possible a more informed critique of how power operates in the political and governance systems (in India) that govern the environment. Such an understanding allows for an ethical and truly meaningful environmental practice in fields such as academia, business, journalism, law, media, policy, etc. Making the quest for environmental and social justice a central concern requires confronting the fundamental underlying processes (and their associated power structures, social relations, institutional configurations, discourses, and belief systems) that generate environmental and social injustices. A coherent political account of how we could respond to these challenges requires the conceptual arsenal provided by environmental justice theory and practice. Ideas of environmental justice therefore enable “new critical engagements with the relations between economy, environment and society, and illuminate the radical potential of sustainability” (Harvey 1996). Such an approach gives environmental practitioners a suitably nuanced, historical, comparative, and ethical orientation for their specific practice. Another question of relevance for this course is: what would distinctly non-Eurocentric environmental justice agendas for India and the global South look like? The analysis here could focus on questions such as: do vulnerable people in India suffer the most from current models of development? If so, why is this and what can be done to correct the situation? How can we balance the tension between economic development and environmental protection in specific Indian geographies? What might be the tensions and polarities between conservationists and resource-dependent communities? What kinds of movements, campaigns, programs and projects are successful in achieving environmental justice in the distinctively Indian contexts of unstable federalism, rampant poverty, violent state action, low literacy, identity and coalition politics, endemic official corruption, poor enforcement machinery, etc.? What can environmental justice practitioners in India learn from the global environmental justice movement and from movements in other places in the global South? Summarizing the above discussion, an environmental justice approach as understood in this course, therefore: a) critically, contextually and holistically re-examines the foundations of environmental/sustainability thinking and action from the standpoint of justice, and; b) applies the insights gained from such a critical re-examination to ethically devise, implement and evaluate new approaches and solutions to pressing socio-environmental problems. The course enables learners to develop an 'environmental justice mindset' and to develop familiarity with some commonly-used approaches, methods, tools and resources to augment the practice of environmental justice in varied sectoral/disciplinary/professional contexts. The course will be built around specific exercises and experiential components focused on professional practice that responds to some key problems of environmental injustice. Workshop-style segments will focus on brainstorming, envisioning, strategising, project design, research methodology, team-work, prototyping, documentation, fund-raising, and communication for professional practice relating to climate change.
