About

Since Adam Smith and David Ricardo, economists have sought to understand the causes, patterns, and consequences of trade among nations. This course introduces students to the key concepts, theories, institutions, and macroeconomic dynamics of international economics. It traces the historical evolution of the global trading system - from Mercantilism and the Gold Standard through Bretton Woods, GATT, and the WTO - and examines why countries trade, what they trade, who gains, and how governments shape trade through policy. Students will engage with the principal theories of trade (Absolute and Comparative Advantage, Heckscher-Ohlin, economies of scale and imperfect competition) and evaluate trade policy instruments including tariffs, quotas, subsidies, anti-dumping measures, and non-tariff barriers. The course also covers the monetary dimensions of international economics: exchange rate regimes (fixed, floating, managed, pegged, spot, forward, and trade-weighted), balance of payments accounting, capital account convertibility, and global capital flows, analyzed through open-economy frameworks including the IS-LM-BOP model. Historical episodes - the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, the oil shocks of the 1970s, the rise of the US dollar as reserve currency - provide context for contemporary debates around the US-China trade conflict, Trump-era tariffs, supply chain disruptions, economic nationalism, industrial policy, digital and services trade, climate-related trade measures, and the geopolitics of regional agreements and groupings such as the QUAD. The course equips students with the analytical tools to navigate the rapidly changing landscape of global economic relations.
