About

This course critically interrogates the notions of ‘democracy’ and ‘autocracy’ with empirical reference to the contemporary world of the early twenty-first century. So often juxtaposed as binary opposites along with related terms—freedom versus tyranny, liberty versus dictatorship—the line between democracy and authoritarianism has become empirically blurred a mere three decades after an American author proclaimed ‘the end of history’ with the ideological triumph of liberal democracy and its likely universalization. In the 1990s, the comparative study of ‘democratization’ was a booming sub-field of political science. In the 2020s, the scales have flipped and the comparative study of ‘autocratization’—also referred to as ‘democratic backsliding’—is a prime preoccupation of political scientists. The abrupt swing of the pendulum shows that the binary distinction between ‘democracy’ and ‘authoritarianism’ in the 1990s and 2000s was an ideal-typical—and ideological—construct. The 2010s and 2020s represent a wake-up call to a far more complicated empirical reality, in which the politics of the leader of the Western/Euro- Atlantic ‘free world’ of democratic nations have taken an unexpected turn, along with that of the ‘world’s largest democracy’ in the non-Western world. The course is particularly focused on what the political scientists Stephan Haggard and Robert Kaufman call ‘the incremental erosion of institutions, rules, and norms that result from the actions of duly elected governments’ (and their leaders). This is a comparative politics course that draws on cases from around the world. The Routledge Handbook of Autocratization (Aurel Croissant and Luca Tomini, eds), published in 2024, is a key course text. It consists of forty chapters organized in five parts.
