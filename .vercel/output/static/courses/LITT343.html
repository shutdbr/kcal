About

Visceral, often uncontrolled and in states of excess or restraint, the body confounds the imagination, transforming and transmuting as it moves through the world. It consumes and is consumed, and is an infinitely malleable entity. The Medieval and Early Modern periods reimagined the relationship between bodies— political, philosophical, spiritual, social and aesthetic— articulating new possibilities for the ways in which bodies manifested themselves and their own positioning within a larger world; one opened up by trade, travel and encounter. In this course, we will explore different understandings and expressions of the body, embodiment, and corporeal subjectivity in European literature from the 6th through 18th centuries. Designed to be interdisciplinary, the course examines discourses of the body and its relationship to questions of biological, chronological, ontological, and epistemic subjectivity. Scholars including Peter Stallybrass and Stephen Greenblatt, have placed the genesis of modern ideas of embodied subjectivity in the early modern period, while Geraldine Heng, JRR Tolkien, Harold Bloom, and others, have theorized the importance of examining medieval discourses of embodiment as precursors to contemporary understandings of race, gender, disability, monstrosity and more. This course draws together scholarship from across disciplines– history, art history, literary studies, performance studies, disability studies, and critical period studies, to complicate, enrich, and diversify how the body becomes and is a site of interrogation and inquiry in the humanities and humanistic social sciences. The course structure is driven by its primary texts, which will change in every iteration. The current iteration of the course focuses on performance and expressive culture, primarily drawn from the Western European canon. Primary readings include Beowulf, the lais of Marie de France, and theatrical texts by Shakespeare, Corneille, and Moliere.
