About

The views of Hume and Kant on morality are arguably the two most significant approaches to moral philosophy during the early modern period. As Thomas Nagel rightly puts it, contemporary moral philosophy “continues to be dominated by the disagreement[s] between [the ethical views of] these two giants” (Nagel, “The Taste of Being Moral”, 2012). It is a well- known fact that Kant initially subscribed to the moral sentimentalist tradition that Hume belonged to, before breaking away as a hardcore moral rationalist. The nature of this decisive break is the starting point between many contrasting perspectives within contemporary meta-ethics and moral psychology – emotivism/cognitivism, moral sense/intuitionism, Humean motivation/Kantian motivation etc. There is therefore a need to backtrack and read Hume’s and Kant’s original contributions to moral philosophy. Through selected primary source readings, the course aims to provide an overview of the central tenets of Hume’s and Kant’s moral philosophy. The course will begin with a useful background on the broader themes in early modern moral philosophy. Students will be then be introduced to Hume’s psychology of action via readings from the first two books of his Treatise. Then, the focus will shift to the core of Hume’s moral philosophy – his anti-rationalism and sentimentalism – that he develops in the third book of the Treatise and in some sections of his second Enquiry. After discussions on Hume’s moral theory, the course will shift its direction towards the rationalist account of morality that Kant propounded as a reaction to the moral sentimentalist tradition. Students will closely engage with Kant’s Groundwork for a deep understanding of the central themes of Kant’s moral theory - practical reason, categorical imperative, autonomy, duty, good will and dignity. Elements of Kant’s moral psychology such as moral motivation, moral incentive, choice and responsibility will also be explored through readings from his second Critique. The course will conclude by highlighting the significance of Hume’s and Kant’s moral theories to contemporary moral philosophy.
